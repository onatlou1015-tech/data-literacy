Part 1

Four Common Methods

When you collect primary data, you can choose from four main methods. Each one is best for different situations.

📝

Surveys / Questionnaires

Asking people questions — usually written, sometimes online. You can collect lots of answers quickly.

Best for: Opinions, preferences, habits of many people at once.
🎤

Interviews

Asking people questions face-to-face. You can ask follow-up questions and get deeper answers.

Best for: Detailed stories, expert opinions, personal experiences.
👀

Observations

Watching and recording what happens — without asking questions. You record what you see or hear.

Best for: Behaviour, counting things, real-life events.
🧪

Experiments

Measuring under controlled conditions. You change one thing and measure what happens.

Best for: Cause and effect, testing ideas, scientific questions.

💡 Key Idea

  • The method must match the question. Not every method works for every task.
  • Surveys and interviews collect what people say.
  • Observations and experiments collect what actually happens.
Part 2

How to Design Good Questions

A data collection is only as good as the questions you ask. Good questions give clear, useful answers. Bad questions confuse people and give weak data.

💬 Clear & Simple

  • Use everyday words.
  • Keep questions short.
  • Ask one thing at a time.

⚖️ Not Biased or Leading

  • Do not push people to answer a certain way.
  • Avoid words like “obviously” or “don't you think”.
  • Offer balanced choices.

📋 Good Answer Choices

  • Yes / No questions.
  • Multiple choice options.
  • Rating scales (1–5).

❌ Bad Question

“Don't you agree that our school canteen food is too expensive and unhealthy?”

Why it's bad: Leading, biased, and asks two things at once.

✅ Good Question

“How would you rate the canteen food on a scale of 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent)?”

Why it works: Neutral, clear, and gives a fair scale.

❌ Bad Question

“What sport do you like?”

Why it's bad: Too open — answers will be hard to compare.

✅ Good Question

“Which of these sports do you enjoy most? (a) Football (b) Basketball (c) Swimming (d) Other”

Why it works: Clear options that are easy to count and compare.

💡 Key Idea

  • If a question is confusing, the answers will be confusing too.
  • Avoid leading questions — they create biased data.
  • Give answer options that cover the possibilities, including “Other” when needed.
Part 3

Types of Answer Choices

The way you ask determines how easy it will be to process the data later. Choose the answer format that fits your question.

✅❌

Yes / No

Quick and easy. Best for simple facts.

“Do you walk to school?”
🔘🔘🔘

Multiple Choice

Choose one option from a list.

“Favorite fruit: Apple / Banana / Orange”
⭐️⭐️⭐️

Rating Scale

Rate something from 1 to 5.

“Rate the canteen food: 1–5”

💡 Key Idea

  • Pick the answer type that gives you data you can count or compare.
  • Yes/No and Multiple Choice are the easiest to tally.
  • Rating scales show degrees of opinion, not just yes or no.
Part 4

Ethics in Data Collection

When you collect data from people, you must be respectful and responsible. Following ethics keeps your data honest and protects the people who help you.

1
🙋

Ask Permission

Always ask before collecting data from someone.

2
🔒

Keep Answers Private

Do not share who said what — keep personal answers confidential.

3
🤝

Be Respectful

Treat everyone politely and never pressure them to answer.

4
🎯

Be Honest

Do not change data to fit what you expected.

🤔 Think About It

  • Why is it important to ask permission before collecting data?
  • What could happen if you shared private answers with everyone?
  • What is unfair about changing data you don't like?
Activity

Let's Practise! 🎯

📝 Activity 1: Choose the Best Method

  1. You want to know the most popular snack in class. Which method is best? Why?
  2. You want to know how many cars pass the school gate in one hour. Which method?
  3. You want to know how the school principal feels about a new sports program. Which method?
  4. You want to know which paper towel absorbs the most water. Which method?

🛠️ Activity 2: Spot the Bad Question

  1. Find the problem: “Don't you love how amazing our library is?”
  2. Find the problem: “How much homework do you get and do you like it?”
  3. Find the problem: “Which is your favorite subject: math, English, science?” (Hint: what if it's not in the list?)
  4. Rewrite each question to make it clear, neutral, and balanced.

✍️ Activity 3: Design Your Own Questions

  1. Choose a topic you want to find out about (e.g., sleep, reading, sports).
  2. Write 3 good questions — using clear words and fair answer choices.
  3. Make sure at least one uses Yes/No, one uses Multiple Choice, and one uses a Rating Scale.
  4. Swap with a partner: check for bias and clarity.

💬 Class Discussion

  • Which method do you think is easiest to use — and why?
  • Which method gives the most accurate results — and why?
  • What is the biggest risk of using a biased question?
Summary

What We Learned Today

📌 Key Takeaways

  • Four main methods: surveys, interviews, observations, experiments.
  • Each method is best for different types of questions.
  • Good questions are clear, simple, and not biased.
  • Answer choices include Yes/No, Multiple Choice, and Rating Scales.
  • Always follow ethics: ask permission, keep answers private, be respectful, be honest.
🎓 Student Outcome: You can now choose a suitable method and design clear, fair questions to collect data.
