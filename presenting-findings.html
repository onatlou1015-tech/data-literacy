Part 1

How to Present Findings Clearly

A good presentation follows a clear structure. Every data story should answer five things in this order:

1
🎯

State the Objective

What did we want to find out?

2
📥

Describe Collection

How did we collect the data?

3
📊

Show the Results

Charts and tables that tell the story.

4
💡

Explain Meaning

What does the data tell us?

5
✅

Draw Conclusions

What should we do next?

💡 Key Idea

  • A clear structure makes your findings easy to follow and remember.
  • Always start with the objective — it gives the audience a reason to listen.
  • End with a conclusion or recommendation — what should happen next?
Part 2

Anatomy of a Data Report

Whether written or spoken, a data report has the same parts. Here is what each section does:

🎯

1. Objective

"Our goal was to find out the most popular fruit in Grade 7."

📥

2. Method

"We surveyed 30 students using a Google Form with 1 question and 5 choices."

📊

3. Results

"Banana was the top choice with 7 votes, followed by Apple (4) and Orange (3)."

💡

4. Meaning

"Banana is the clear favourite — nearly twice as popular as Orange."

✅

5. Conclusion

"For a healthy snack day, we should offer bananas as the main option."

💡 Key Idea

  • Every data report — written, spoken, or visual — should include these five parts.
  • Keep each part short and clear. Don't overload the audience.
  • Data is persuasive when it is paired with a clear message.
Part 3

Presentation Formats

You can present your findings in different ways. Each format works best for a different situation.

📖

Written Report

Best for detailed analysis. Can be shared and re-read.

📊

Slide Presentation

Great for speaking to a group. Uses visuals and short text.

🖼️

Poster

Shows your findings at a glance. Good for display boards.

🎤

Oral Presentation

Talking through your findings live. Engaging and personal.

💡 Key Idea

  • Choose the format that fits your audience and your time limit.
  • A slide presentation usually combines visuals and speaking.
  • A poster works well when people walk past and read on their own.
Part 4

Matching the Presentation to the Audience

The same data can be presented differently for different audiences. Always ask: Who am I presenting to?

👧 Presenting to Younger Students

  • Use big, colourful charts
  • Keep language simple
  • Use pictograms and icons
  • Short sentences and clear numbers

🧑‍💼 Presenting to Teachers or Adults

  • Use detailed charts and tables
  • Include method and sample size
  • Give percentages and averages
  • End with a recommendation

💡 Key Idea

  • The same data can be shown in different ways depending on who is listening.
  • Good presenters adjust their message to their audience.
  • Always think: What does my audience need to know?
Part 5

Telling the Story Behind the Data

Data becomes powerful when you tell the story behind it. Compare the two examples below.

😴 Just Data

"We surveyed 30 students. Banana got 7 votes. Apple got 4. Orange got 3."

Boring — just a list of numbers. No meaning.

✨ Data with a Story

"We wanted to find the top snack for our class. After asking 30 students, one fruit stood out — banana, chosen by nearly one in four of us. So for our next healthy snack day, bananas should be the star."

Engaging — tells the objective, the result, and what to do next.

💡 Key Idea

  • Numbers alone are not a story. Meaning and action make it one.
  • Start with why you collected the data.
  • End with what should happen next.
Activity

Let's Practise! 🎯

📝 Activity 1: Structure the Story

  1. Use the fruit survey data: Apple 4 · Banana 7 · Orange 3.
  2. Write one sentence for each of the five parts: Objective, Method, Results, Meaning, Conclusion.
  3. Share it with a partner and compare structures.

🛠️ Activity 2: Match the Format

  1. Which format would you choose if you had 2 minutes to present?
  2. Which format for a parent visiting the classroom?
  3. Which format to send to the principal for review?
  4. Explain your reasoning for each choice.

✍️ Activity 3: Rewrite the Story

  1. Here is boring data: "We asked 20 students. 12 said yes, 8 said no."
  2. Rewrite it as an engaging story with a purpose and a conclusion.
  3. Swap with a partner to compare styles.

💬 Class Discussion

  • Why does the order of the five parts matter?
  • How would you change your presentation for a group of teachers vs. a group of Grade 3 students?
  • What makes a data presentation convincing and memorable?
Summary

What We Learned Today

📌 Key Takeaways

  • Present data using five parts: Objective → Method → Results → Meaning → Conclusion.
  • Choose a format that fits your audience: report, slides, poster, or talk.
  • Match your presentation to who is listening.
  • Turn numbers into a story — start with why, end with what's next.
🎓 Student Outcome: You can now present data findings clearly and accurately, in a way that fits your audience.
