Part 1

What Does "Evaluating" Mean?

Evaluating means judging whether data is good, useful, and trustworthy. It is not enough to just collect and process data — you must also ask: Can I believe this? Does it really answer my question?

🤔 Why Evaluate?

  • Bad data leads to bad decisions.
  • Data can be misleading — even if it's real.
  • Someone may have collected it unfairly.

🎯 What to Do

  • Ask critical questions.
  • Spot common problems.
  • Decide whether to trust the data.

💡 Key Idea

  • Evaluating is the third step of the data cycle: Collect → Process → Evaluate → Present.
  • Good data detectives question everything.
  • Only trustworthy data should be presented to others.
Part 2

Five Questions to Ask When Evaluating

Whenever you look at data — whether it's yours or someone else's — run through these five checks.

🎯

Accurate?

"Was it measured correctly?"

Were the tools, questions, and methods reliable? Any mistakes in counting?

📋

Complete?

"Did we collect enough?"

Are there missing values? Did everyone answer? Is the sample big enough?

🔗

Relevant?

"Does it answer our objective?"

Does the data actually match the question we set out to answer?

⚖️

Fair?

"Were any groups left out?"

Was the sample balanced? Or was one group over-represented?

📅

Current?

"Is it recent enough?"

Was the data collected recently? Old data may no longer apply.

💡 Key Idea

  • If the answer to any of these questions is "no," the data may not be reliable.
  • Ask all five — one weak point can undermine the whole dataset.
Part 3

Spotting Problems in Data

Data can be misleading or weak in four common ways. Learning to spot these is the heart of evaluating.

⚖️ Bias in Questions

Questions that push people to answer a certain way.

“Don't you think our canteen food is too expensive?”
Fix: Ask neutral questions — “How would you rate the canteen food?”

👥 Small Sample Size

Asking too few people — the results don't represent the whole group.

“We asked 3 students, and they all said they love math.”
Fix: Collect data from a bigger, more balanced group.

🔍 Missing or Inconsistent Data

Some answers are missing, or the data was recorded in different ways.

One column has "85" as a number, another has "eighty-five" as text.
Fix: Use one clear format, and check for missing entries.

📊 Misleading Graphs

Charts that exaggerate or hide the truth by changing the scale or cutting axes.

Starting a bar chart at 90 instead of 0 — tiny differences look huge.
Fix: Always start bar charts at 0, and label axes clearly.

💡 Key Idea

  • These four problems are the most common reasons data can be misleading.
  • Always ask: Could this data have been collected or shown better?
Part 4

Visual Example: Misleading vs. Honest Graph

The same numbers can look very different depending on how you draw the chart. Compare these two:

⚠️ Misleading Graph

80
82
85
Bar chart starting at 75 — small differences look huge!

✅ Honest Graph

80
82
85
Bar chart starting at 0 — shows the true difference.

💡 Key Idea

  • Always check the scale of a chart before believing it.
  • Bar charts must start at 0 to be honest.
  • A misleading graph can make a tiny change look enormous.
Activity

Let's Practise! 🎯

📝 Activity 1: Spot the Problem

  1. “We asked 2 students if they like homework, and both said no.” → What's the problem?
  2. “Do you agree that our school day is too long and boring?” → What's the problem?
  3. A bar chart comparing test scores starts at 90 instead of 0. → What's misleading?
  4. One column says “85,” another says “85 points,” and another says “eighty-five.” → What's the problem?

🔍 Activity 2: Evaluate Real Data

  1. Find a chart or survey result from a website or news article.
  2. Ask the 5 questions: Accurate? Complete? Relevant? Fair? Current?
  3. Write a short review: Would you trust this data? Why or why not?

✍️ Activity 3: Fix the Data

  1. Your class collected data from only 5 people. How could you make it fair?
  2. A survey uses the question “Don't you love our amazing new library?” Rewrite it fairly.
  3. You have 30 answers, but 4 are missing. What should you do before processing?

💬 Class Discussion

  • Why is evaluating data important before sharing it with others?
  • Can data be accurate but still misleading? Give an example.
  • What could happen if we skip the evaluating step?
Summary

What We Learned Today

📌 Key Takeaways

  • Evaluating = judging whether data is good, useful, and trustworthy.
  • Ask five questions: Accurate? Complete? Relevant? Fair? Current?
  • Watch for four common problems: biased questions, small samples, missing data, misleading graphs.
  • Always check the scale of charts — bar charts must start at 0.
  • Only trustworthy data should be presented to others.
🎓 Student Outcome: You can now evaluate the quality and reliability of data and spot common problems.
